Sourdough fermentation depends on a stable partnership between wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. The two groups tolerate each other because they consume different sugars and because the acid produced by the bacteria suppresses competitors. This balance is surprisingly robust once established.

A mature starter typically contains between one and ten billion bacterial cells per gram, outnumbering yeast cells by a factor of about a hundred. Despite this imbalance, the yeasts contribute most of the gas that raises the dough. Their relative slowness is compensated by the long bulk fermentation.

Temperature is the most effective control the baker has. Warmer dough favours the bacteria, producing a sharper, more acidic loaf, while cooler conditions shift the balance towards the yeasts and a milder flavour. A difference of three degrees changes the result noticeably.

Hydration influences both texture and microbial activity. Wetter doughs ferment faster because diffusion is easier, but they also weaken the gluten network and demand more careful handling. Most bakers settle somewhere between seventy and eighty percent.

Flour composition determines what the organisms have to work with. Whole grain flours carry more minerals, more enzymes and a larger native microbial population, which is why refreshing a sluggish starter with rye so often revives it. White flour ferments more predictably but less vigorously.

The timing of shaping matters as much as the timing of mixing. Dough shaped too early lacks structure, while dough shaped too late has already spent much of its gas. Experienced bakers judge the moment by feel rather than by clock.

Baking transforms the accumulated chemistry in a few minutes. Steam keeps the surface flexible during the initial expansion, after which the crust sets and browning reactions dominate. The characteristic aroma comes largely from compounds formed in this final stage.

Storage reveals a practical advantage of the method. Naturally leavened bread stales more slowly, because the acids produced during fermentation slow the retrogradation of starch. The effect is measurable for several days.

Reproducibility remains the hardest goal for a commercial bakery. Ambient conditions vary, flour deliveries differ between batches, and the starter itself drifts over months. Written records of temperature and timing are the only reliable way to trace a disappointing batch back to its cause.

None of this requires laboratory equipment. A thermometer, a scale and consistent notes are enough to turn an unpredictable craft into a repeatable process.
